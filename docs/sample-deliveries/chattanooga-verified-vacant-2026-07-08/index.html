The actual question you asked

"What's the difference between your data and what I already have?"

Fair question — you didn't ask for a sales pitch, you asked what's different. So here it is straight: most vacant-land lists on the market get compiled once, sold many times, and never rechecked. The list you're texting today might be six, twelve, eighteen months old. In that window, a real share of it stops being true — someone broke ground, someone sold, or the county's own vacancy flag was wrong to begin with (assessor systems misclassify parcels more than people assume). You keep paying for outreach against land that isn't actually available anymore.

What a stale purchased list gets you

  • A snapshot from whenever it was last compiled — could be a year or more old
  • No way to tell which parcels already sold or got built on
  • Vacancy flags taken at face value from the source file, errors included
  • No signal on which owners are actually likely to sell vs. just holding

Where it sits geographically

Not just "Chattanooga" — the current top-scored working file breaks down by area.

In the current highest-priority working file, the split runs roughly: County North and County South unincorporated parcels carry the largest share, with the city of Chattanooga itself next, and smaller pockets in Soddy-Daisy, Signal Mountain, Collegedale, Red Bank, and East Ridge. That means this isn't a one-neighborhood list — it can be narrowed to whichever part of the metro fits your build/hold strategy before delivery.

Absentee ownership — the motivated-seller angle

Out-of-state owners show up from a real spread of states.

Among the flagged absentee owners in the current working file, the heaviest out-of-state concentration comes from Georgia and North Carolina, with additional owners spread across Florida, Virginia, Texas, Kentucky, and beyond. An owner who bought Hamilton County land but lives states away is a fundamentally different conversation than a local holder — that's the segment worth calling first.
